We are committed to feeding children impacted by COVID-19.

When schools closed in 2020, the meal that millions of children depended on disappeared overnight. Community organisations stepped in. The lessons of that moment still matter.

For millions of children in the United States and around the world, breakfast and lunch at school were not just routine. They were the most reliable meals of the day. When school buildings closed in March 2020 to contain the spread of COVID-19, those meals disappeared at the same moment that household incomes were collapsing. The result, briefly and visibly, was a hunger crisis.

Across the country, school districts, food banks, faith communities, and small charities responded with extraordinary speed. Within weeks, grab and go meal distribution was operating at thousands of sites. Buses became delivery vehicles. School cafeterias kept cooking, sending meals out the door rather than serving them indoors. The improvisation worked. The lessons it taught are worth holding onto.

The scale of the crisis

At the peak of the disruption, food insecurity in households with children spiked sharply. National survey data showed double digit percentage increases in the share of families reporting they sometimes or often did not have enough to eat. The pain was unevenly distributed. Black and Hispanic households, single parent households, and households in low wage service work bore the heaviest burden.

Federal emergency programs, including Pandemic Electronic Benefits Transfer and universal free school meals, made an enormous difference. By the summer of 2021, food insecurity in households with children had declined by roughly seven percentage points compared to early 2020.

How community organisations adapted

The organisations that responded fastest were the ones already embedded in their communities. Food banks pivoted from warehouse based pantry models to direct distribution at schools, churches, libraries, and community centres. Volunteers replaced staff in some sites because paid workers were sick or sheltering. Drivers learned new routes. Cooks scaled recipes for grab and go portion control.

What made these efforts work was not heroism alone. It was logistics, trust, and the existence of pre built relationships. Where a charity already had a partnership with a local school or library, food started moving in days. Where those relationships did not exist, every meal took longer.

The pandemic did not invent childhood hunger. It revealed how thin the safety net always was.

Lessons that outlast the emergency

Three lessons stand out. First, school meals are essential infrastructure, not a discretionary nicety, and they need to be funded as such. Second, community based organisations are the connective tissue that makes federal programs reach the families who need them most, and those organisations need sustained operating support rather than just project grants. Third, the simplest and most dignified way to feed a hungry child is to make meals universally free, no application required, no questions asked.

Several states have moved to permanent universal free school meals since the pandemic, and the early evidence is exactly what advocates predicted. Participation rises, stigma falls, and the administrative burden on families and schools shrinks.

What commitment looks like now

The acute phase of the COVID-19 emergency has passed, but childhood hunger has not. Inflation, the end of pandemic era benefits, and rising housing costs have pushed many families back into food insecurity. Commitment, in this season, means resisting the urge to declare victory. It means keeping summer feeding programs fully funded, sustaining afterschool meal sites, and protecting the federal and state programs that proved their worth.

The community organisations that fed children through the worst of the pandemic are still here. Backing them, year after year, is how we honour the promise that no child in our communities should learn, sleep, or grow up hungry.
