COVID-19 causes a sharp rise in deaths of NYC homeless.

Some 613 homeless New Yorkers died between July 2019 and June 2020, a 52 percent jump on the previous year. The numbers tell a story of a system that was already fragile.

The first wave of COVID-19 in New York City was brutal for almost everyone. For homeless New Yorkers, it was catastrophic. According to data compiled by the Coalition for the Homeless and reported by Gothamist, 613 homeless New Yorkers died between July 2019 and June 2020, a 52 percent increase compared to the previous fiscal year. The largest number of those deaths happened between April and June, the precise window when the virus tore through the five boroughs.

Of that total, around 120 deaths were confirmed or probable COVID-19 cases. The mortality rate among sheltered homeless New Yorkers reached 436 deaths per 100,000 people, roughly 49 percent higher than the citywide rate. Across the United States, homeless individuals were estimated to be two to three times more likely than the general population to die from COVID-19.

Why the shelter system was so vulnerable

The reasons are structural. Many congregate shelters in New York rely on shared sleeping rooms, shared bathrooms, and dining halls. Social distancing in those environments is essentially impossible. A virus that spreads through respiratory droplets in crowded indoor spaces had, in many shelters, near perfect conditions to spread.

The population itself also carried a heavier underlying disease burden. Homeless adults experience higher rates of chronic illness, including diabetes, hypertension, and respiratory disease. Many had limited access to consistent primary care. When COVID-19 arrived, it found a population already medically vulnerable and an environment that worked against any attempt to contain it.

What the city tried

New York moved, eventually, to relocate thousands of single adults from congregate shelters into hotel rooms, with the aim of creating physical distance and reducing transmission. Public health teams scrambled to test, vaccinate, and treat shelter residents. Frontline outreach workers continued the slow, patient work of building trust with people sleeping on the street.

These interventions saved lives. They also exposed how the system had been built. A network designed primarily for cost efficiency, dependent on shared rooms and large dormitories, was not a network designed to survive a respiratory pandemic.

The virus did not discriminate, but the system did. People without a door of their own died at a far higher rate than people with one.

The harder year that followed

In 2021, the picture changed in a troubling way. Total deaths among homeless New Yorkers rose to roughly 640, about four percent higher than 2020. COVID-19 deaths declined as vaccines rolled out, but overdose deaths surged. Drug related deaths in the homeless population climbed from around 131 in fiscal year 2020 to 237 the following year, driven largely by fentanyl in the street drug supply.

2021 became the deadliest year on record for homeless New Yorkers. The crisis had shifted shape, but it had not ended. Where COVID-19 had been the headline killer in 2020, the overdose crisis took that grim role in 2021, with COVID-19 still contributing.

What lasting reform would look like

Advocates have called for a sustained shift away from large congregate shelters toward private rooms, expanded supportive housing for adults with mental illness or substance use disorders, better integration between street outreach and primary care, and aggressive funding for harm reduction services.

None of these solutions are mysteries. The cost of inaction is measurable in body counts, year after year. The pandemic made the cost of a fragile shelter system impossible to ignore. The question now is whether the city and the state will treat the lessons of 2020 and 2021 as a generational warning, or as a chapter to be quietly closed.
