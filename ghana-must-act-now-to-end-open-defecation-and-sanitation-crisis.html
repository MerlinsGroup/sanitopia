News / Ghana Must Act Now to End Open Defecation and Sanitation Crisis

Ghana must act now to end open defecation.

One in every four households in Ghana still practises open defecation. The economic cost is staggering, the human cost is worse, and the time for incremental progress is over.

Ghana has built roads, expanded mobile networks, and grown a sophisticated middle class. Yet on one of the most basic measures of public health, the country has barely moved in a decade. Roughly 25 percent of Ghanaian households, or one in every four, still practise open defecation. In some northern regions the rate climbs as high as 49 percent. Only about 24 percent of Ghanaians have access to basic sanitation services, well below the national policy target of 39 percent.

This is not a story about poverty alone. It is a story about prioritisation, planning, and political will. The technology to fix sanitation is well known. The financing is achievable. What has been missing is sustained urgency.

The economic case is overwhelming

Ghana's leaders have themselves acknowledged the scale of the loss. Poor sanitation is estimated to drain the national economy of more than 500 million US dollars every year, with open defecation alone costing around 79 million dollars annually. That figure is not abstract. It shows up in lost school days, in medical bills, in productivity lost to preventable illness, and in tourism revenue that never materialises.

By contrast, the investment required to build a school sanitation block, or to subsidise household toilets in rural communities, is a fraction of those losses. Every cedi spent on sanitation returns multiples in avoided healthcare costs and recovered economic output.

The human case is even stronger

Diarrhoea related illness, much of it driven by poor sanitation and contaminated water, kills around 3,600 Ghanaian children every year. Roughly 15 percent of maternal deaths in Ghana are linked to infections that better sanitation would help prevent. Schoolgirls miss days of class every month because their schools do not offer private, safe facilities. The gender gap in education widens with every missed lesson.

A nation that aspires to middle income status cannot accept that one of its citizens in four still has nowhere safe and private to go.

What acting now actually looks like

Ending open defecation in Ghana will not happen by accident. It needs a coordinated plan with three legs. First, infrastructure investment, prioritising schools, health facilities, and rural communities where the deficit is largest. Second, behaviour change campaigns that work with communities rather than lecturing them, so that newly built facilities actually get used and maintained. Third, accountability mechanisms, so that local government and contractors are held to delivery standards that survive past a single political cycle.

The diaspora has a role to play. Ghanaian professionals living abroad, alumni networks of schools that shaped them, and corporate partners with a stake in the country's growth can all accelerate progress through targeted philanthropy and impact investment. Sanitopia itself was founded on exactly that logic.

The cost of waiting

Every year that Ghana defers action, thousands of children fall sick, hundreds die, schoolgirls drop out, and hundreds of millions of dollars evaporate from the economy. The arithmetic of waiting is brutal, and it grows worse with every passing budget cycle.

The good news is that this is one of the most solvable problems in development. The targets are clear, the technologies exist, and the people are ready. What is required now is the political and civic courage to treat sanitation as the urgent national priority it has always been.
